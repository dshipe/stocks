# Lessons from a $140K Loss

## Overview

One of the most candid and widely referenced posts on Kullamägi's blog, this piece walks through a significant $140,000 single-trade (or series of trades) loss, what went wrong, and what practical risk management lessons emerged. It exemplifies his philosophy that **transparency about losses is as important as celebrating wins**.

---

## What Went Wrong

The post identifies a combination of compounding mistakes — the same sequence that catches many traders off guard even after years of experience:

1. **Position too large relative to account:** The trade size was too big, meaning a moderate adverse move produced a catastrophic dollar loss even with a relatively tight stop percentage.
2. **Moving the stop:** Rather than honouring the original stop-loss level, the position was held (or the stop was widened) as the trade moved against expectations — a classic and deadly mistake.
3. **Averaging down into a loser:** Adding to a losing position ("buying the dip") instead of exiting amplified the damage.
4. **Ignoring market context:** The broader market may have been deteriorating, but conviction in the individual name overrode objective assessment.
5. **Emotional attachment:** Once a large position is held through a significant drawdown, the sunk-cost fallacy often kicks in — making it psychologically harder to cut the loss.

## Key Risk Management Lessons

### 1. Honour Your Stops — Every Single Time
Your pre-trade stop is set when your mind is **clear and rational**. Once a trade is on, emotion clouds judgment. Moving or ignoring stops is almost always a mistake.

### 2. Position Size Defines Dollar Risk, Not Just Percentage
Risk a fixed **percentage of account equity per trade** (Kullamägi typically uses 0.25–1%). If your stop is further away, use fewer shares. Never let one trade threaten your account.

### 3. Never Average Down on a Loser
Adding to a losing position increases risk precisely when the market is telling you that you are wrong. The correct response to a losing trade is to exit, not to double down.

### 4. Accept Losses as the Cost of Doing Business
Every trader — including the most successful — will have large losses. What separates professionals from amateurs is **keeping losses within planned limits** and not letting a single trade spiral into an account-threatening event.

### 5. Have a Written Trade Plan Before Entry
Define:
- Entry price and trigger
- Stop-loss level (non-negotiable)
- Target or scale-out plan
- Maximum position size

Stick to the plan. Deviating from a pre-defined plan mid-trade is almost always driven by emotion, not logic.

### 6. Take Time Off After a Large Loss
Large losses produce a psychological state that impairs future decision-making. Stepping away from the screen, reviewing what went wrong, and resetting mentally is more productive than immediately trying to "make it back."

### 7. Review and Adjust Systematically
After a significant loss, perform a **post-mortem**:
- Where exactly did you deviate from your rules?
- Was this a setup failure or an execution failure?
- What specific change to your process will prevent a repeat?

## The Broader Context

Kullamägi is unusual among highly successful traders for his willingness to document and publicise major losses. He believes that **honest self-analysis and public accountability** are more educational — for himself and for the community — than a carefully curated highlight reel of winners.

The $140K loss, while painful, did not threaten his overall account because his general risk management practices kept most positions sized conservatively. The lesson is that even experienced traders can make these mistakes in isolated trades — the goal is to build systems that make catastrophic outcomes structurally impossible.
